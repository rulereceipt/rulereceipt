Why an outside check

The case for an independent record, and how we know this tool is accurate

Why an outside check

An agent grading its own work has the same problem a company auditing its own books does. That's structural — it doesn't go away as models improve.

Anthropic makes the architectural case themselves. Their April 2026 paper Trustworthy agents in practice splits agent security into four layers — model, harness, tools, environment — and notes that "a well-trained model can still be exploited through a poorly configured harness, an overly permissive tool, or an exposed environment." Three of those four layers belong to whoever deploys the agent, not the model vendor. They describe what's needed as "the kind of infrastructure no single company can build alone."
The gap is measured, not assumed. A Cloud Security Alliance study published April 2026 found 53% of organizations had AI agents exceed their intended permissions — and only 8% said it never happens. In the same research, more than two-thirds reported they can't clearly tell an agent's actions apart from a human's. That last one is the whole problem in a sentence: you can't review what you can't distinguish.
Where the rules are heading. Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI (January 2026, the first of its kind) asks for a verifiable identity per agent and an audit trail of which agent acted under whose authorization. It's voluntary guidance, not enforcement — and the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations were pushed to December 2027, not this year. We'd rather say that plainly than manufacture a deadline. The direction is clear even where the dates aren't.
How we know it's accurate — because we got it wrong first. We ran this against a real session and it reported ten rule violations. Every one was wrong. Rather than patch them, we found the cause: a text match can never prove someone did something — searching a session for a banned command finds it whether the agent ran it, looked it up, or mentioned it in a note. So we rebuilt it to read what actually happened, and a text match can no longer report a failure at all. Where it isn't certain, it says so instead of guessing. The whole thing is written up, including the wrong turn we took fixing it: what we got wrong.
Tested against real rules files, not our own examples. 559 public rules files — CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md, .cursorrules, Copilot instructions, Windsurf and Gemini — from PyTorch, Kubernetes, Elasticsearch and hundreds of other projects, covering 23,704 parsed items and 8,808 real rules, with no crashes. When that corpus nearly doubled with formats the tool had never been tuned on, its behaviour barely moved. And it's tested the other way too: sessions that deliberately break a rule, to confirm it catches them, not just that it stays quiet on good ones.

Figures above are dated on purpose — they're from specific 2026 studies and will age. The structural argument is what we'd stand behind regardless: an independent record beats a self-report, whatever the current numbers say.
